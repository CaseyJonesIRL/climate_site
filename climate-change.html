A study and a petition are not the same kind of evidence

One side of this argument points to a review of 88,125 peer-reviewed climate papers published from 2012 to 2020, which found a consensus of more than 99% that humans are causing climate change (Environmental Research Letters, 2021). The other points to petitions signed by tens of thousands of people, including the 1998 Oregon Petition. These aren’t comparable. A petition is a signature. A peer-reviewed paper is data, method, and analysis that other scientists tried to find fault with before it was allowed to publish, and can still challenge after.

What’s well-established, and what isn’t

“If it disagrees with experiment it is wrong.”
— RICHARD FEYNMAN, THE CHARACTER OF PHYSICAL LAW (1965) · WATCH HIM SAY IT →

Well-established

  • CO2 traps heat — measured directly since Tyndall’s 1859 experiments. See the mechanism →
    Venus has a CO2-thick atmosphere and is the hottest planet in the solar system — hotter than Mercury, which is closer to the sun. Distance doesn’t explain that. CO2 does.
  • Ice cores show CO2 cycled between roughly 180–300 ppm over the last 800,000 years. It’s now above 420 ppm — already outside that entire natural range, reached in a geological instant. Live CO2 data →
  • Most of ExxonMobil’s own internal projections, made between 1977 and 2003, accurately predicted the warming that has since been observed (Science, 2023).
  • Warmer air holds more water vapor — about 7% more per 1°C. Basic physics, not a projection.

Still being worked out

  • The exact rate of sea-level rise beyond the next few decades — model spread is genuinely wide. Boston tide-gauge trend →
  • Cloud feedback effects — by climate scientists’ own account, one of the largest remaining sources of uncertainty. IPCC synthesis, not original research →
  • Precise regional and local impacts — much harder to pin down than global averages.
  • Whether the total number of hurricanes is increasing — the historical data doesn’t support a clear trend either way (NOAA’s own assessment →). Intensity is a different story: the share of storms reaching Category 3–5 has likely increased over the past four decades, though tying that specifically to human-caused warming carries only medium confidence (IPCC AR6, Chapter 11 →).
