A study and a petition are not the same kind of evidence
One side of this argument points to 88,125 peer-reviewed papers (2012–2020) — 99.9% concluding human activity is the primary driver. The other points to petitions signed by tens of thousands of people, including the 1998 Oregon Petition. These aren’t comparable. A petition is a signature. A peer-reviewed paper is data, method, and analysis that other scientists tried to find fault with before it was allowed to publish, and can still challenge after.

What’s well-established, and what isn’t
“It doesn’t matter how beautiful your theory is, it doesn’t matter how smart you are. If it doesn’t agree with experiment, it’s wrong.”
— RICHARD FEYNMAN
Well-established
  • CO2 traps heat — measured directly since Tyndall’s 1859 experiments. See the mechanism →
    Venus has a CO2-thick atmosphere and is the hottest planet in the solar system — hotter than Mercury, which is closer to the sun. Distance doesn’t explain that. CO2 does.
  • Ice cores show CO2 cycled between roughly 180–300 ppm over the last 800,000 years. It’s now above 420 ppm — already outside that entire natural range, reached in a geological instant. Live CO2 data →
  • The fossil fuel industry’s own internal models from the 1970s predicted the warming that’s since been observed (Science, 2023).
  • Warmer air holds more water vapor — about 7% more per 1°C. Basic physics, not a projection.
  • Boston Harbor’s tide gauge shows measurable sea-level rise since 1825, accelerating since the mid-20th century (JGR: Oceans, 2018 — published by the American Geophysical Union).
Still being worked out
  • Whether hurricane frequency (not intensity) is increasing — the literature doesn’t support a clear claim either way. NOAA’s own assessment →
  • The exact rate of sea-level rise beyond the next few decades — model spread is genuinely wide. Live trend data →
  • Cloud feedback effects — by climate scientists’ own account, one of the largest remaining sources of uncertainty. IPCC synthesis, not original research →
  • Precise regional and local impacts — much harder to pin down than global averages.
